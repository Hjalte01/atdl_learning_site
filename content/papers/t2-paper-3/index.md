## 1 · The puzzle: change the decision, preserve the person

A face classifier says “not smiling.” What visible change would make it say “smiling” while preserving the person and most of the photograph? Changing a few imperceptible pixels might flip the prediction, but it would explain little to a human. Replacing the whole face might be realistic, but it would explain little about this particular input.

DiME combines a generative image prior with the gradients of the **fixed classifier being explained**. Its target is the opposite of that classifier’s original binary prediction. The explanation is a generated alternative image, not proof of what would happen under a real-world causal intervention.

[![Three goals: flip the classifier, preserve the input, and remain a plausible image.](/figures/dime/problem.svg)](/figures/dime/problem.svg)

Read the three boxes together: a successful explanation must balance all three goals. Diversity adds another goal—there may be several plausible edits. This is a schematic, not an example generated by DiME.

This guide follows the supplied **19-page ACCV 2022 paper by Guillaume Jeanneret, Loïc Simon, and Frédéric Jurie**, proceedings pages 858–876. The [conference record](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ACCV2022/html/Jeanneret_Diffusion_Models_for_Counterfactual_Explanations_ACCV_2022_paper.html) confirms its identity; all method details and numbers below come from the [local course PDF](/papers/dime.pdf). No later journal-version results are mixed in.

**A useful reasoning path (teaching interpretation):** use diffusion to keep edits plausible; start from a partially corrupted input to preserve structure; evaluate the classifier on clean reconstructions because it was trained on clean images; then transfer those gradients back to the noisy sampling state without differentiating the entire denoising chain.

## 2 · Training and inference have different jobs

[![Training fits an unconditional diffusion model on faces; explanation time freezes it and the target classifier and changes only the image state.](/figures/dime/training.svg)](/figures/dime/training.svg)

The upper path learns a face distribution. The lower path uses that prior to explain an already trained classifier. Green denotes image data or state, purple denotes a learned network, and orange denotes guidance. These colors recur below. The diagram is functional, not a layer-by-layer reconstruction.

The source uses CelebA at **128 × 128** resolution and explains a **DenseNet121** classifier on the binary Smile and Young attributes. Its unconditional DDPM uses the referenced ImageNet unconditional architecture with internal channels reduced from 256 to 128, trained for 270,000 iterations, batch size 75, learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.05 (§3.2). The paper delegates the detailed diffusion training objective to the cited DDPM work; DiME’s central contribution is the explanation-time guidance, not a new denoising training loss.

The generator is trained using images without attribute labels. This does **not** mean the target classifier is untrained or the whole experimental pipeline is label-free. A separate attribute oracle is used for evaluation. No target-classifier or diffusion weights are updated while explaining an individual image; gradients are taken with respect to image values.

<details><summary>Predict: would retraining the classifier on noisy images explain the same fixed model?</summary>

Not necessarily. DiME’s task is to explain an existing classifier. Adapting its weights would change the model under investigation. Instead, DiME changes what input the classifier sees during guidance.

</details>

## 3 · Representation: noisy images, not compressed face codes

The editable state $z_t$ is a noisy image with the same spatial/channel shape as the input $x$, conceptually $128\times128\times3$ RGB values here. The paper calls noise levels latent spaces; this does not imply a VAE bottleneck or a text-token representation.

[![The image-shaped state combines retained image signal and Gaussian noise; more corruption leaves less input information.](/figures/dime/representation.svg)](/figures/dime/representation.svg)

The coefficients are measured mathematically; the illustrated noise levels are teaching choices, not the paper’s actual schedule. At the initialization arrow, paper Eq. 2 can be sampled as

$$
z_\tau=\sqrt{\alpha_\tau}\,x+\sqrt{1-\alpha_\tau}\,\epsilon,
\qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad
\alpha_t=\prod_{k=1}^{t}(1-\beta_k).
$$

Here $\tau$ is the chosen starting step, $t$ indexes diffusion time, $\beta_k$ is the forward noise variance at step $k$, $I$ is the identity covariance, and $\epsilon$ is image-shaped standard Gaussian noise. **The paper’s $\alpha_t$ is the cumulative product** often written $\bar\alpha_t$ elsewhere.

Starting at an intermediate $\tau$ preserves some information about this face. Starting at full noise would weaken that connection; starting too close to the clean input can leave too little freedom to change the attribute. This tradeoff is an interpretation, not a guaranteed monotonic relationship between noise and explanation quality. The paper uses 500 diffusion steps for its model, respaces to 200 at test time, and starts editing at $\tau=60$ in that sampling setup.

## 4 · Full architecture: look ahead to a clean image

[![A noisy state branches to a full unconditional clean rollout and to a reverse transition; classifier and proximity gradients from the clean rollout shift the transition mean.](/figures/dime/architecture.svg)](/figures/dime/architecture.svg)

Follow A → B → C → D → E. From current noisy state $z_t$, the fixed DDPM **rolls all the way to a clean image** $x_t$. The subscript on $x_t$ identifies which outer state produced that clean rollout; it does not mean $x_t$ is noisy. This is not a single denoiser call that directly predicts the clean image.

At box C, the desired label and closeness to the original input compete. Paper Eq. 5 writes

$$
L(z_t;y,x)=\mathbb E[\widetilde L(x_t;y,x)],\qquad
\widetilde L=\lambda_c L_{\mathrm{class}}(C(y\mid x_t))
+\lambda_p L_{\mathrm{perc}}(x_t,x).
$$

$C(y\mid x_t)$ is the fixed classifier’s probability for target label $y$; $L_{\mathrm{class}}$ encourages that target and $L_{\mathrm{perc}}$ penalizes perceptual departure from reference $x$. The nonnegative weights $\lambda_c$ and $\lambda_p$ set their relative influence. The expectation is over stochastic clean rollouts. The implementation uses **one realization per outer step**, not a large Monte Carlo average. The authors suggest averaging effects across steps; this is not a guarantee of low estimator variance.

The clean-image gradient $g_x=\nabla_{x_t}\widetilde L$ says which image changes reduce this combined objective. The main text leaves the component losses abstract here; the guide does not silently substitute a particular classifier loss or perceptual backbone. Increasing the classifier weight can help flip the label while sacrificing proximity.

The practical implementation also adds $\eta\|z_t-x\|_1$, a direct noisy-state proximity term with $\eta=0.05$. Away from zero differences, its noisy-state gradient is $\eta\,\operatorname{sign}(z_t-x)$; this term does not pass through the clean rollout. The source uses $\lambda_p=30$ and tries $\lambda_c\in\{8,10,15\}$, increasing it if a run fails to flip the decision.

## 5 · Where the approximation enters

The mathematically exact chain rule for a fixed rollout realization would multiply by the entire rollout Jacobian (paper Eq. 6):

$$
g_z=\left(\frac{D x_t}{D z_t}\right)^{\!\top}g_x.
$$

$D x_t/D z_t$ describes how every clean output changes when the noisy input changes. Carrying this derivative through all reverse steps requires a deep computation graph. DiME avoids that memory cost using the forward corruption relation as a surrogate.

If the noise $\epsilon$ is held fixed in paper Eq. 7, solving for the clean image gives the **teaching derivation**

$$
x_t=\frac{z_t-\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\epsilon}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{D x_t}{D z_t}=\frac{I}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}.
$$

Thus box D transfers the gradient using paper Eq. 8:

$$
\widehat g_z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}g_x.
$$

The paper prints an equality but explicitly describes this as an **approximation**. The derivative is exact for the fixed-noise algebraic surrogate, not generally for the learned multi-step reverse rollout. Matching forward and reverse distributions does not prove equality of their pathwise Jacobians. This distinction is essential to understanding both the efficiency and the limitations of DiME.

At box E, the DDPM’s reverse mean $\mu(t,z_t)$ and covariance $\Sigma(t,z_t)$ define a plausible next-state distribution. Paper Eq. 4 shifts its mean in a loss-decreasing direction:

$$
z_{t-1}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(
\mu(t,z_t)-\Sigma(t,z_t)\widehat g_z,\;
\Sigma(t,z_t)\right).
$$

The covariance scales guidance as well as sampling noise. This is a mean shift, not a weight update. The practical direct $\ell_1$ gradient can be included in the noisy-state guidance in addition to $\widehat g_z$. When $\alpha_t$ is very small, the surrogate gradient scale becomes large; the formula is not defined at $\alpha_t=0$.

<details><summary>Predict: has DiME made every reverse step cheap by avoiding the rollout Jacobian?</summary>

It reduces backpropagation memory, but still performs many forward denoiser calls to create clean rollout images. The paper reports roughly 1,800 DDPM evaluations per explanation (§4.6).

</details>

## 6 · Follow one scalar through the boxes

[![A toy pixel is corrupted to 0.76; clean gradient 0.4 becomes 0.5; variance 0.04 shifts mean 0.70 to 0.68 before a sampled next state of 0.70.](/figures/dime/numbers.svg)](/figures/dime/numbers.svg)

This is a synthetic arithmetic example, not a DiME prediction. At initialization choose $x=0.8$, $\alpha_t=0.64$, and noise $\epsilon=0.2$. Box A receives $z_t=0.8(0.8)+0.6(0.2)=0.76$.

Suppose box B’s full rollout yields a clean candidate whose combined loss at C has gradient $g_x=0.4$. Its clean candidate value and gradient are hypothetical outputs, not quantities determined by the single noisy pixel. Box D computes $\widehat g_z=0.4/0.8=0.5$.

Suppose E’s reverse mean is $\mu=0.70$ and scalar **variance** is $\Sigma=0.04$. Ignoring the optional direct $\ell_1$ term for clarity, the guided mean is $0.70-0.04(0.5)=0.68$. With a fresh standard-normal draw $\xi=0.1$, the sampled next state is

$$
z_{t-1}=0.68+\sqrt{0.04}(0.1)=0.70.
$$

The variance scales the mean shift, while its square root scales the random draw. A sampled step need not decrease the loss even if its mean is shifted downhill. The example does not clip values or run a trained model.

## 7 · The outer loop and its inner rollouts

[![Three outer noisy states are carried forward, each using a separate rollout to a clean candidate to refresh guidance.](/figures/dime/steps.svg)](/figures/dime/steps.svg)

Horizontal arrows carry the evolving noisy image. Each downward branch is an unconditional look-ahead rollout used to evaluate the current candidate. These clean previews provide guidance; they do not replace the noisy state at every outer step. This is a schematic with three steps, not the experiment’s $\tau=60$ schedule.

A conceptual traversal of Figure 1 and Eqs. 4–8 is:

1. Keep the original image $x$ fixed; choose opposite prediction $y$, a starting noise level, and a classifier weight.
2. Corrupt $x$ to obtain $z_\tau$.
3. At each descending outer time, obtain a clean candidate from the current noisy state with unconditional reverse sampling. Evaluate its classifier/proximity gradient and transfer that gradient using Eq. 8.
4. Sample the next noisy state using the shifted reverse mean. Carry that state forward and refresh the clean candidate for the next guidance step.
5. Finish at a clean output and check whether the prediction flipped. If it did not, retry with the next configured classifier weight. Exhausting the three weights is a failure.

**Source notation caveat:** §3.2’s summary paragraph interchanges clean-image subscripts while describing cached gradients and refreshed rollouts. The diagram and traversal here use $x_t$ consistently for the clean rollout associated with $z_t$, as introduced before Eq. 5. They explain the mechanism rather than claim line-for-line code equivalence or resolve undocumented initialization details.

Why not stop immediately when a clean preview flips the class? The early-stopping ablation finds nearly the same flip rate but worse image metrics; the authors also observe low-confidence examples close to the decision boundary. Finishing the trajectory can matter for the quality of the explanation.

## 8 · Evidence: success and quality must be read together

[![Reported Smile FID values are 29.4 for DiVE, 36.8 for DiVE100, and 3.17 for DiME; lower is better.](/figures/dime/results.svg)](/figures/dime/results.svg)

These bars reproduce **Table 1’s Smile FID** on CelebA at 128 × 128. They are measured source values; the chart is newly drawn. FID summarizes distribution-level image statistics, not the causal faithfulness of an individual explanation. These quality metrics use successful counterfactuals, so the success rate affects which images enter evaluation.

| Method | Smile FID ↓ | Smile FVA ↑ | Smile MNAC ↓ | Young FID ↓ | Young FVA ↑ | Young MNAC ↓ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| DiVE | 29.4 | 97.3 | Not reported | 33.8 | 98.2 | 4.58 |
| DiVE100 | 36.8 | 73.4 | 4.63 | 39.9 | 52.2 | 4.27 |
| DiME | 3.17 | 98.3 | 3.72 | 4.15 | 95.3 | 3.13 |

FVA is the percentage whose face-embedding cosine similarity exceeds 0.5 using a ResNet50 pretrained on VGGFace2. MNAC counts changed attributes predicted by an oracle fine-tuned on CelebA. Neither establishes that the classifier used only the visible feature a reader notices.

In §4.1, DiME’s flip rates are **97.6% Smile and 98.9% Young**. The authors recomputed DiVE and obtained only 44.6% on Smile; extending optimization to 100 steps (DiVE100) raised success to 92.0% Smile and 93.4% Young. The differing successful subsets complicate the FID comparison. DiME does not win every metric: Young FVA is lower than DiVE’s 98.2%.

The separate mini-validation ablation in Table 2 isolates guidance choices more directly:

| Variant | What supplies guidance? | Flip rate (%) ↑ | FID+ ↓ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Direct | Classifier on noisy state, without reciprocal-alpha scaling | 19.7 | 50.51 |
| Naive | Classifier on the original image every time | 70.0 | 98.93 ± 2.36 |
| Early stopping | DiME, stopped as soon as a preview flips | 97.3 | 51.97 ± 0.77 |
| DiME | Refreshed clean-image guidance through the trajectory | 97.9 | 50.20 ± 1.00 |

FID+ matches the number of successful examples across variants. Its values should not be compared directly with Table 1 FID. Direct changes both the classifier’s input and gradient scaling, so this comparison alone cannot isolate the contribution of scaling. A proposed follow-up would hold the input type fixed and ablate only that scale.

For diversity (§4.3), five independent runs produce reported LPIPS-based scores of 0.213 for DiME versus 0.044 and 0.086 for the DiVE comparison and its Fisher Spectral variant. This evaluation uses a small validation subset and includes failed explanations, unlike the main quality metrics. **Reading caution:** Eq. 11 prints $2/[n(n+1)]$ for a sum over $j<k$; a conventional average over unordered pairs would use $2/[n(n-1)]$. The values here are quoted as reported, not recomputed or silently renormalized. Diversity alone does not establish validity.

## 9 · Why fewer changed attributes can mislead

If a smile classifier relies on a correlated feature, a counterfactual may change that feature too. MNAC penalizes every extra changed attribute, even when the change helps expose a learned association. Low MNAC is therefore not automatically stronger evidence of faithful explanation.

[![Correlation Difference compares dataset correlations with correlations of oracle-predicted attribute changes; toy differences 0.10 and 0.05 sum to 0.15.](/figures/dime/correlation.svg)](/figures/dime/correlation.svg)

The diagram is a two-attribute teaching example, not the reported CelebA result. For target attribute $q$, other attribute $a$, method $M$, image $X$, and oracle prediction $O_a$, define the change

$$
\delta^M_{q,a}(X)=O_a(M(X,q))-O_a(X).
$$

Across images, compare the Pearson correlation of target/other changes with the ground-truth label correlation:

$$
c^M_{q,a}=\operatorname{PCC}(\delta^M_{q,q},\delta^M_{q,a}),\qquad
c_{q,a}=\operatorname{PCC}(Y_q,Y_a),\qquad
\mathrm{CD}_q=\sum_a|c_{q,a}-c^M_{q,a}|.
$$

$Y_q$ and $Y_a$ are binary ground-truth attributes; PCC is Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The first equation operates on original/counterfactual pairs, and the final sum is the evaluation box in the diagram. Smaller CD means closer agreement with the dataset’s correlation pattern. Paper Eq. 9 motivates it by observing that differences between **independent** pairs of labeled samples preserve their correlation: differencing doubles both covariance and the two variances. Counterfactual pairs are not independent draws; substituting their oracle changes is a metric design choice, not the same theorem.

For the toy diagram, $|0.6-0.5|+|-0.2-(-0.15)|=0.15$. The source reports CD **2.30 for DiME versus 2.33 for DiVE100** on CelebA validation (§4.2), a much smaller separation than their MNAC scores might suggest.

PCC requires nonzero variance; an attribute that never changes makes the naive correlation undefined. The paper does not specify a zero-variance convention here. Oracle errors and generator biases also affect CD. Matching observed correlations does not demonstrate causal dependence or prove exactly which feature the target classifier uses.

<details><summary>Predict: a method changes only the target attribute and has excellent MNAC. Has it ruled out spurious correlations?</summary>

No. It may simply fail to reveal associated changes. Compare success, realism, preservation and correlation behavior, then test the classifier with controlled interventions before making a stronger claim.

</details>

## 10 · Limits and the idea to carry forward

DiME is expensive: the paper reports approximately 1,800 DDPM calls per explanation. Avoiding rollout backpropagation saves memory without removing those forward calls. It also requires access to training images for its generative model, and access to classifier input gradients; “post-hoc” here does not mean a query-only black-box interface.

The experiments concern faces and two attributes. The authors document changes to pendants and difficulty reconstructing unusual shapes such as hands (§4.4). A plausible generated face may erase real unusual details. The lower Young identity score is consistent with more distributed edits, but the authors explicitly leave that explanation as a hypothesis needing further study. No result here guarantees minimal edits, identity preservation, or causality for arbitrary images.

Useful next experiments would compare the surrogate with exact rollout gradients on short chains, vary $\tau$ while measuring both flip rate and identity, and repeat CD with alternative oracles and uncertainty estimates. These are proposed tests, not findings of this paper.

**The idea to carry forward:** when explaining a model trained on clean inputs, use a clean view to ask what should change, then carefully account for how that guidance reaches the generative state. A shortcut in that transfer is part of the method’s assumptions. Evaluating the resulting explanations requires more than counting successful flips or changed features.
